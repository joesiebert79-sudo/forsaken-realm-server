# Forsaken Realm Chronicles — Book One
## Chapter Three: The Names Beneath the Stone

### Page One — The Price of a Name

The first thing the traveler noticed was that the blood on the steps had begun to climb.

It gathered itself into thin red threads and crept uphill, against the pull of the earth, toward the broken crown. The bandit clerk pressed his sleeve to his mouth. His eyes had returned to their ordinary brown, but the terror in them had not.

“Put it back,” he said.

“I haven't taken it.”

“Then why is it following you?”

The traveler looked down. One half of the crown had shifted across the stone. Its jagged edge pointed toward the traveler like the needle of a compass.

Beyond the open archway, the staircase descended into a darkness that swallowed the light of their lantern after seven steps. Somewhere below, water dripped in a slow, deliberate rhythm.

“What's your name?” the clerk asked suddenly.

The traveler had given a dozen names at a dozen border crossings. None seemed safe here.

“Why?”

“Because something down there is trying to remember it.”

The lantern dimmed. From beneath the steps came a sound like fingernails tracing letters on stone.

### Page Two — The Watch Captain's Oath

At Thornhaven's northern gate, Captain Edric Voss watched a merchant caravan turn around for the third time that morning.

The horses would not cross the old road. They reared whenever the awakened spire came into view, though its silver markings had faded to a patient blue. A child sitting atop a flour wagon pointed toward the hill.

“It's counting us,” she said.

Edric followed her gaze. The tower's light brightened once for each person passing beneath the gate, then fell still.

He sent two riders to the hill and ordered the gates kept open. Thornhaven had survived famine and war by refusing to become a prison to its own people. He would not change that for a tower.

At the blackthorn tree in the square, an old mason named Halren caught his sleeve.

“My grandfather helped seal that spire,” Halren whispered. “He said the builders didn't fear what might come out. They feared what the world would do when it learned what had been put inside.”

“What was inside?”

“Names, Captain. Names the kings agreed to bury.”

Then the tower rang once, without a bell.

### Page Three — The Archivist's Debt

Lady Seraphine stood before the mountain spire, shivering though the morning air was warm.

The valley lay revealed within her mind: every river bend, every hidden ford, every road erased from modern charts. Yet one patch of the vision remained black. It was shaped like a burial mound beneath the ice.

She placed her palm against the tower.

“I answered your question,” she said. “What more do you want?”

Silver writing appeared beneath her fingers. Not in the ancient language of the Seven Kingdoms, but in the hand of her mother, who had died when Seraphine was eleven.

*You did not leave because you were afraid. You were sent away because you heard him.*

Seraphine staggered back. She remembered a winter corridor, a locked door, and her mother kneeling to whisper: Never repeat the name you hear beneath the mountain.

She had spent her life believing she fled that night. Now the memory was changing.

A second line formed.

*Find the seventh witness before the king finds the seventh grave.*

The script vanished. Seraphine knew, with dreadful certainty, that the tower had not invented those words. It had returned them.

### Page Four — Seven Seats, Six Seals

Beneath Ravenshollow, the traveler descended three steps before the clerk seized the back of their coat.

“Look at the walls.”

Seven niches lined the staircase. In six stood stone kings with their faces deliberately chiseled away. The seventh niche was empty. At its base, an inscription had survived the hammer:

*He kept the door closed from the inside.*

The traveler held up the lantern. Beneath each ruined statue was a different seal: river, flame, stag, sword, sun, thorn, and a crown split through its center.

The last seal had been struck not by a chisel, but by something that had burned the stone from within.

The clerk began to weep. “I know this place.”

“You've been here before?”

“No.” He touched the river seal. “I remember ordering men to wall it shut. I remember their names. I remember the sound they made when the mortar covered their mouths.”

The traveler backed away. The clerk was scarcely thirty years old. The masonry had stood for centuries.

From below came the same patient voice.

“Memory is not inheritance. It is a debt.”

### Page Five — The King's Command

In Vaeloria, King Aldric dismissed his council before the noon bells.

His generals wanted troops sent to Thornhaven. His priests demanded the spires be broken. His treasurer asked who would pay for either course. Aldric listened to them all and gave no answer.

When the chamber emptied, he unfolded a letter that had arrived without messenger or seal. It contained a single drawing: a silver crown broken in two.

On the reverse were seven words.

*The buried king remembers what you promised.*

Aldric held the parchment to a candle. It would not burn.

He had heard the voice beneath the Hollow Crown for years. At first it spoke of harvests, peace, and roads safe for children. Only lately had it begun to speak of necessary sacrifices. He had told himself a wise ruler must sometimes choose the lesser evil.

Now he wondered who had taught him to measure evil that way.

He summoned his oldest guard.

“Bring Lady Seraphine home,” he said. “Quietly. And send no priest with you.”

“Is she accused of something, Majesty?”

Aldric stared at the unburning letter.

“Not yet.”

### Page Six — The Third Light

High above the northern valleys, a shepherd boy named Tovin woke to find seven crowns drawn in the ashes of his family's hearth.

He remembered drawing them in his sleep. He did not remember why one had been erased.

Outside, the mountain spire glowed across the snow. A second light flickered far below, beyond the forest, where no tower should have been visible. Between them lay a ridge of black rock.

The ridge opened like an eye.

For one breath, a third spire appeared inside the mountain itself: narrow, pale, and wrapped in chains of light. Tovin heard a man's voice speaking from impossibly far away.

“Do not wake the last one until they know why I stayed.”

Then the ridge was stone again.

His grandmother stood in the doorway, pale as winter milk. She had heard the voice too.

“Who was he?” Tovin asked.

She shut the door and laid the iron bar across it.

“In my mother's stories,” she said, “he was the only king who refused to be saved.”

Far below, the first two spires burned brighter, as though answering a call neither had meant to send.

### Page Seven — The Name That Was Taken

The traveler reached the bottom of the stair alone.

The clerk had stopped halfway down, whispering apologies to people long dead. Beneath Ravenshollow lay a circular chamber with seven doors. Six were sealed by iron plates. The seventh stood open onto a wall of living darkness.

In the center waited a stone table. Upon it lay a book bound in gray leather, its pages blank except for a single name written again and again. The traveler leaned close.

Every time they tried to read it, the letters changed.

The split crown lifted from the staircase above. Its two halves floated through the chamber and hovered over the book, trembling as if some unseen hand struggled to join them.

“Who are you?” the traveler demanded of the darkness.

A figure appeared behind the seventh door: a man wearing chains instead of royal robes. His face remained hidden, but his voice was human, weary, and terribly near.

“I am not what they buried,” he said. “I am what they left awake.”

“Tell me your name.”

“If I do, the other six will hear.”

Above them, every bell in Ravenshollow began to ring. In Thornhaven, Captain Voss drew his sword. On the mountain, Seraphine saw the black space in her remembered map spread toward the valley.

The chained figure raised one hand.

“Find the witness,” he whispered. “Before the king opens the wrong grave.”

And for the first time in centuries, the seventh door began to close.
