# Forsaken Realm Chronicles
## Book One — The Age of Splendor
### Chapter Three — The Names Beneath the Stone

The first thing the traveler noticed was the silence.

Not the ordinary silence of an abandoned cellar, where water dripped between stones and rats scratched behind the walls. This was something deeper. It seemed to press against the ears, swallowing the sounds of breathing, clothing, and footsteps until the traveler could hear only the slow, uneven beating of their own heart.

Above them, Ravenshollow continued its nightly business. Men gambled away stolen silver. Drunken mercenaries argued over ale. Somewhere in the town, a woman was probably cursing her husband for coming home without his wages.

But beneath those crooked streets, in a chamber older than any building standing above it, a silver crown lay broken upon the stone.

And something in the darkness was waiting for an answer.

The bandit clerk stood three steps above the traveler, holding a lantern in one trembling hand. He was a narrow-shouldered man whose ink-stained fingers looked better suited to counting stolen coins than handling a sword. Until tonight, the traveler had considered him little more than a frightened accomplice to Ravenshollow's petty cruelties.

Now his face had gone pale, and his eyes kept returning to the crown.

“Don't touch it again,” he whispered.

The traveler crouched beside the silver.

The crown had been fashioned with extraordinary care. Seven narrow points rose from its band, each worked into the likeness of a different creature. A stag stood beside a serpent. A wolf faced a bird with its wings spread wide. Other figures had been worn nearly smooth by centuries of neglect.

Across the front ran a deep fracture, dividing the crown into two unequal pieces.

Yet the broken edges were not jagged.

They were perfectly smooth.

As though the crown had not been broken by force, but separated by design.

“Who brought this here?” the traveler asked.

The clerk swallowed.

“I don't know.”

“Someone must.”

“No one comes down here. Not anymore.”

The traveler looked over one shoulder.

“Not anymore?”

The clerk hesitated.

Then, almost too quietly to hear, he said, “There used to be a rule.”

“What rule?”

“That if you heard someone calling your name from beneath Ravenshollow, you weren't supposed to answer.”

The traveler straightened.

“And who made that rule?”

“My grandfather told it to my father. My father told it to me. I always thought it was something they invented to keep children away from the old tunnels.”

He glanced toward the darkness.

“Until tonight.”

The flame in the lantern leaned sideways, though no wind touched their faces. The traveler took it from his hand and held it lower.

Light revealed shoulders where there should have been wall, bowed heads pressed close, hands resting on shoulders that weren't quite there. Dozens, maybe hundreds, all facing the crown.

The clerk dropped to his knees.

“What did it say?” the traveler asked, though he already knew the answer would cost them.

The clerk's mouth worked soundlessly before words came.

“My mother's name.”

He shook his head hard as if to fling the sound away, but it clung.

The lantern flame bent again. The crowd of shadows did not move, and the crown began to glow with tired silver like moonlight across a grave.

From far below, a second voice whispered, “Six were permitted to forget.”

The traveler felt those words like chains settling on skin.

Then: “The seventh was condemned to remember.”

The clerk screamed.

Above them, a bell began to ring without being struck.

The traveler grabbed the clerk's collar and hauled him upright.

“Move!”

But the clerk's boots dragged as if memory had weight.

“They're calling me,” he sobbed. “They're calling all of us.”

The traveler didn't answer. He stared at the shadows one heartbeat longer and understood the cruel thing.

The voice wasn't offering warning or mercy.

It was counting witnesses.

He dragged the clerk toward the stairs. Behind them, the crown pulsed once, soft as breath, and the shadows leaned after them without moving.

When they burst into the night, Ravenshollow was still laughing, still trading.

But every dog in town had gone silent, and every child who could speak woke with a name on their tongue they were too afraid to say.
